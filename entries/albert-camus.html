Albert Camus, and the Ticket He Wouldn't Buy

Key text: The Myth of Sisyphus (1942)
Meaning Death Control
Lived1913–1960, Algeria and France (46 years, killed in a car crash)
Known forThe Stranger, The Myth of Sisyphus, The Plague, The Rebel
PioneeredAbsurdism as its own thing, separate from existentialism, whether or not people believed him

Everyone owns a Camus quote. "Life is absurd" gets tattooed on ribcages, printed on tote bags, dropped into a bio next to a photo of someone smoking on a rooftop. Almost none of those people have read the guy. I'm not saying that to be superior about it either, because for years I was one of them, quoting a man I'd never actually sat with. Then I did, and it turns out the tote bag version is doing him dirty. What he actually built is one of the most useful things I've ever read, and it starts with a boulder.

Quick version of the myth: Sisyphus, a king, tricks death, gets caught, and the gods punish him with a task built to be pointless on purpose. Push a boulder up a mountain forever. Every time it nears the top it rolls back down and he starts again. No end, no finish line. They didn't pick torture, they picked something worse: labor that goes nowhere.

Camus looks at that and asks the obvious question nobody wants to ask out loud: isn't that just a Tuesday? You wake up, go to work, come home, do it again. The bills come back. The dishes come back. You'll floss again tomorrow having flossed today. Camus didn't invent that feeling, he just didn't look away from it, which is more than most people managed before him.

That's the absurd, and it's the part people get wrong most. It's not "life is meaningless," full stop, sad trombone. It's a collision. Your brain wants the universe to explain itself. The universe doesn't answer. Not cruelly, just silently. The absurd is the friction between those two things, and you're standing right where they rub together.

Camus thought people mostly deal with that friction two ways, and he wasn't a fan of either. One is what he called philosophical suicide: you can't find the meaning, so you invent one and pretend you found it. Religion can do this. So can nationalism, astrology, a hustle, a relationship you've turned into a religion of its own. You paper over the silence and agree not to look at the seams. The other option is the literal one, and Camus opens the whole book saying it's the only philosophical question that actually matters: if life might be meaningless, why not just end it? He takes it seriously enough to spend a whole book answering it, which tells you where he lands.

His answer is revolt. Not revolution, nobody's setting a building on fire. Just revolt in the quiet sense: you see the absurd clearly, you refuse both the fake meaning and the exit, and you keep pushing the boulder anyway, eyes open. Not resignation, not delusion. A third thing. You do the pointless thing on purpose, and the fact that you keep doing it becomes the point.

Here's where it gets less tidy, and where most explainers stop short. Camus wasn't just misread by strangers on the internet decades later, he got misread badly by the people closest to him, while he was alive to feel it. He spent years being lumped in with Jean-Paul Sartre as an existentialist, a label he flat out rejected. "No, I am not an existentialist," he told an interviewer in 1945, more or less exhausted by having to say it. Then in 1951 he published The Rebel, which argued that revolutionary violence, including the kind happening under Stalin, curdles into the same tyranny it claims to fight. Sartre and most of the French left at the time took that as a betrayal. The friendship ended publicly, in print, in essays that read like a breakup nobody wanted witnesses to. Camus spent the rest of his life being called naive by people who thought revolution excused almost anything, and a little too political by people who wanted their absurdist author to stay in his lane. He never really got to land anywhere comfortable while he was alive to enjoy it.

Then in 1957 he won the Nobel Prize in Literature at forty-four, one of the youngest recipients ever, and instead of it feeling like vindication, it seems to have unsettled him. He wrote to a friend around that time describing something closer to dread than triumph, like the honor had made him a bigger target rather than a settled name. Three years later he was dead, in a car his publisher was driving, on a trip he'd almost skipped in favor of the train. He reportedly had the train ticket still in his pocket. A philosopher of the absurd, killed by a genuinely absurd coincidence, which is the kind of detail you couldn't put in a novel because nobody would believe you.

I think about the boulder part more than almost anything else I've read, because my job puts me in it daily. I run speaker cable through the walls of houses owned by people who could buy my apartment building and not notice the withdrawal. I crawl through attics in July so somebody's home theater sounds right. None of it matters in a hundred years, and honestly not much in five. There was a stretch where that thought nearly hollowed me out, standing on a ladder thinking about the heat death of the universe while holding a drill. Camus is who talked me down off that ladder. Not by telling me it matters. By telling me it doesn't have to, and I get to keep going anyway, and the going is mine.

The line everyone quotes and almost nobody sits with is the last one: "One must imagine Sisyphus happy." Not that he is happy. That we have to imagine it, actively, on purpose, because the alternative is pretending the boulder will someday stay at the top, and it won't. Sisyphus knows that mountain better than anyone alive. Every crack, every angle, every spot his foot lands right before the rock gets away from him again. That knowledge is his, and nobody can take it, even though it never gets him anywhere. Camus isn't telling you to smile through the meaninglessness. He's telling you the boulder is yours precisely because it goes nowhere.

The boulder was never going to stay. Push it anyway, and mean it.
