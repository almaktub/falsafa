Immanuel Kant, and the Walk He Never Missed

Key text: Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785)
Control Meaning
Lived1724–1804, almost entirely in and around Königsberg, Prussia, never traveling more than about a hundred miles from home (79 years)
Known forGroundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Critique of Pure Reason, the categorical imperative
PioneeredAn ethics built on universal duty instead of outcomes or feelings, would this still work if literally everyone did it

The story gets repeated so often it's practically folklore at this point, and it's at least partly true: Kant's daily walk through Königsberg was so exactly timed that neighbors reportedly set their clocks by it, and he's said to have broken the routine only once in his entire life, so absorbed reading Rousseau's Emile that he lost track of the hour entirely. He never traveled more than about a hundred miles from the town he was born in, his whole life, and built from that narrow, repetitive, almost monkish existence one of the most influential ethical systems Western philosophy has ever produced. There's something worth sitting with in that alone, that the most far-reaching moral framework in the canon came out of a man who barely left his own neighborhood.

Before he ever got to ethics, he'd already rearranged how philosophy thought about knowledge itself, in the Critique of Pure Reason, in a move he compared directly to Copernicus flipping the earth and the sun. Philosophers before him mostly assumed the mind is a passive receiver, the world simply pours its true structure into your senses and you record it, like a camera. Kant argued the mind isn't passive at all, it actively structures everything it receives, space, time, causality, aren't properties sitting out there in the world waiting to be discovered, they're the built-in shape your mind imposes on raw experience before you're even aware of doing it. You never get access to the world as it actually is in itself, the noumenal world, only the world as your own mind has already organized it for you, the phenomenal world. That's a genuinely unsettling claim once you sit with it, that some of what feels like bedrock reality is actually furniture your own mind installed before you walked in.

Here's the actual argument, stripped of the textbook density it usually gets buried under. Before you act, Kant says, ask a specific question: could the rule behind this action work as a universal law, followed by literally everyone, in every similar situation, without the whole system collapsing under its own logic. Take lying. It's tempting in the moment because it's convenient, this one specific lie, this one time, gets you out of something uncomfortable. But run the test: what if everyone lied whenever it was convenient. Trust collapses entirely, and the moment trust collapses, lying stops even working as a strategy, because nobody believes anything anyone says anymore. The convenience of your one lie was quietly borrowing off a general norm of honesty it would help destroy if everyone followed your same rule. That's the categorical imperative in its first, most famous form: act only according to a rule you could will to become universal.

The second formulation matters just as much and gets quoted less: treat people always as ends in themselves, never merely as a means to your own end. Not that you can never ask anyone for anything, but that you can't treat another person as purely a tool for what you want without regard for what they want, their own reasoning, their own goals. Both formulations point at the same underlying refusal, Kant doesn't care whether the lie or the shortcut would have worked out fine this one time. The point isn't the result. The point is the rule you were willing to act on, and whether that rule could survive being applied by everyone, to everyone, always.

This is duty-based ethics, deontology, and it sits in deliberate contrast to judging an act purely by its outcome. A lot of my trade quietly runs on the outcome logic instead, and it's tempting for exactly the reasons Kant flags. Nobody's ever going to open that wall again. The extra twenty minutes it takes to run the cable properly instead of the fast, sloppier way costs me time nobody will ever bill for and nobody will ever notice missing. The outcome-only version of ethics says, correctly in a narrow sense, that cutting the corner here changes nothing measurable. Kant's question is different and harder to dodge: would this be fine if every installer, on every job, cut this same corner whenever nobody was watching. Once you ask it that way, the whole industry's reputation is quietly riding on an answer you don't actually want to give out loud.

I don't know that I live by his framework as cleanly as he lived by his own walking schedule. Almost nobody does, that's part of the honest criticism of him, the theory can get rigid past the point people can actually sustain. But the question itself, not whether this gets away with it, whether this would still be fine if it were the rule everyone followed, has quietly ruined a few convenient shortcuts for me since I first ran into it, and I mean that as a compliment.

Nobody's ever going to check the wall. Do it right anyway, because the rule you're actually following is the one that would hold up if everyone was watching.
