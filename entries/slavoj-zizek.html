A QUESTION TO READ WITH

Why can seeing through an illusion fail to change how we act?

Žižek has a bit he does, in interviews, in lectures, in the middle of otherwise serious books, where he explains a piece of Hegel using a Hitchcock movie or a Coke commercial or a joke about toilets, three different national styles of toilet, actually, and how each one reveals a different culture's relationship to waste and ideology. People clip these moments and pass them around as though he's a comedian who wandered into a philosophy department. He is being funny. He is also, every single time, making an argument you'd miss if you only watched for the punchline.

The argument he keeps returning to, across a genuinely enormous and repetitive body of work, is about ideology, and it starts by rejecting the version of ideology most people were taught. One familiar account of ideology treats it as false consciousness: they don't know what they're doing, and if you showed them the truth, the mask would come off and they'd see clearly. Žižek's contribution, borrowed from Lacan and Peter Sloterdijk and pushed further, is that this isn't how it works anymore, if it ever fully did. His formula is: they know very well what they are doing, and they are doing it anyway. You know the influencer lifestyle is manufactured. You buy the sneakers regardless. You know the office job is, structurally, extracting more from you than it returns. You show up Monday anyway. The mask isn't hiding the truth from you. You're already holding the truth. The mask is doing something else, letting you keep acting as though you don't, which turns out to be the actual load-bearing function of ideology.

That's why the person most thoroughly inside an ideology, in his account, is often the one loudly announcing they've escaped it. "I'm not political, I just call it like I see it." "I don't believe in any of that, I'm just being realistic." Žižek's whole method is to treat that exact sentence as the tell, not the exemption. The person who thinks they're standing outside the frame, looking at it objectively, is usually the one most fully arranged by it, because the frame's cleverest trick is convincing you there's a neutral spot to stand where no frame applies. He's less interested in telling you what to believe instead than in making that blind spot visible, again and again, in whatever cultural object happens to be lying around, a movie, an ad, a toilet.

Worth knowing where the performance came from. Žižek grew up in Tito's Yugoslavia, a socialist state that was officially non-aligned and often held up abroad as a gentler alternative to Soviet communism, and he's spent a good part of his career arguing that growing up inside that specific system, ostensibly free but still saturated with unspoken rules about what could and couldn't be said, is exactly what trained his eye for ideology's quieter mechanisms, the ones that don't announce themselves as propaganda. He ran, unsuccessfully, for a place on Slovenia’s collective presidency in 1990 as Yugoslavia was breaking apart, a real, concrete brush with electoral politics that rarely makes it into the version of him built entirely out of lecture clips.

Underneath the performance is real philosophical weight, mostly German and mostly difficult on purpose. He's spent decades arguing that Hegel has been badly misread as a philosopher of neat synthesis, thesis meets antithesis, tidy resolution, when the actual Hegel is closer to a philosopher of permanent, productive contradiction, gaps and failures that don't get smoothed over so much as revealed as necessary. Layered on top of that is a Lacanian account of desire that argues you don't actually want the object you think you want, you want the wanting itself, and getting the object usually just relocates the want somewhere else rather than satisfying it. It's a genuinely dark claim about human motivation if you sit with it, and it explains a lot about why getting what you asked for so rarely feels like getting what you asked for.

The showmanship draws real criticism, and it's worth naming rather than excusing: some serious readers of Hegel and Lacan think he trades rigor for spectacle, that the jokes are load-bearing in the wrong direction, holding up an argument's popularity rather than its actual structure. I think that's a fair fight to have, and I don't think it fully cancels what he's doing, which is dragging genuinely hard continental philosophy into a register normal people will actually sit through.

I think about the "they know, and they do it anyway" formula more than I expected to, mostly around subscriptions and warranties, my own industry's bread and butter. I've sold extended coverage to a client while both of us silently understood the math rarely favors them, and they bought it anyway, and I'd be lying if I said I fully understood why until I read Žižek putting a name on it. Nobody in that transaction was fooled. The not-being-fooled was never the obstacle.

You already know. That was never the part that was going to save you.

KEEP THE QUESTION OPEN

A distinction worth keeping.

The claim concerns practices and social arrangements as well as private belief. Buying something while doubting its advertising is an illustration, not sufficient evidence for a complete theory of ideology. Žižek’s readings of Hegel and Lacan are interpretations, not neutral summaries.
