Thinker · Bukhara/Persia, 980–1037 CE

Ibn Sina, and the Man Floating in Nothing

Key text: Kitab al-Shifa (The Book of Healing), the Flying Man argument
About 5 minutes · A personal reading
Self & Consciousness Meaning

A QUESTION TO READ WITH

Would you know you existed without sensing your body?

The name of this site matters here. Falsafa is an Arabic word for philosophy, borrowed from Greek. The traditions it names developed through translation, argument and new work across a wide scholarly world. That history includes Muslim, Christian and Jewish thinkers; it did not begin from nothing, end after five hundred years, or leave everyone in Europe doing nothing in the meantime. Ibn Sina belongs near the center of this story, as a philosopher as well as a physician.

Lived980–1037 CE, Bukhara and greater Persia (birth date disputed)
Known forThe Canon of Medicine (a major medical reference in parts of Europe for centuries) and Kitab al-Shifa, The Book of Healing
Central ideaThe Flying Man argument on self-awareness, six centuries before Descartes' cogito, plus a systemized version of Aristotelian logic that shaped philosophy in both the Islamic world and, later, medieval Europe

Quick facts first, because they're absurd enough to need stating plainly. He had memorized the entire Quran by age ten. He'd surpassed his teachers in medicine by his late teens. He wrote that medical encyclopedia, the Canon, while also working as a court physician and vizier for a rotating cast of unstable rulers, occasionally getting imprisoned, occasionally going into hiding, writing some of his most important philosophical work from memory because he didn't have access to his library. If your idea of a philosopher is a guy calmly pacing a garden, Ibn Sina is closer to someone writing his thesis between assassination attempts.

Here's the actual argument, and it's the reason he's here and not just a history footnote. About six hundred years before Descartes sat by his fire and decided the one thing he couldn't doubt was that he was doubting, therefore he existed, Ibn Sina posed a different thought experiment about immediate self-awareness. The comparison is striking, but the arguments have different starting points and purposes. Picture a person created instantly, fully grown, fully rational, but suspended in total darkness, floating, with no sensory contact with anything at all. No sight, nothing to see. No touch, his limbs don't touch each other or anything else. No sound, no smell, nothing. Total sensory blankness. This is the Flying Man.

Would this man, in that state, know that he exists?

Ibn Sina says yes, immediately, no hesitation, and that's the whole argument. Even stripped of every sense, every piece of information about his body or surroundings or history, he'd still be aware of himself, aware of his own awareness, aware there's a someone doing the not-sensing. That awareness isn't built from sensory data, because he has none. It's not inferred from evidence, because there's nothing to infer from. It's just there, immediately, the one thing that can't be stripped away because it's the thing doing the stripping. His conclusion: the self, the soul, whatever you want to call the thing that is you, isn't identical to the body. That is the conclusion he wants the thought experiment to illuminate. Whether self-awareness without awareness of the body establishes a nonbodily soul remains a philosophical question, not a demonstrated clinical fact.

The Flying Man wasn't a standalone party trick either, it sat inside a much larger logical system he'd built out of his own reworking of Aristotle. Ibn Sina spent real effort tightening and extending Aristotelian logic, refining how syllogisms handle modal claims, statements about what's necessary, possible, or impossible rather than just what's flatly true or false, work precise enough that later logicians in both the Islamic world and, once translated, medieval Europe treated his versions as improvements on the Greek original rather than mere commentary. The Flying Man is what that logical rigor looks like when pointed at the self instead of at syllogisms, the same insistence on finding the one thing that survives every possible stripping-away, applied to the hardest object there is.

I read this the first time on a break at work, sitting in a customer's driveway between jobs, and it stopped me completely. Not because I necessarily buy the metaphysics, whether the soul's really separable from the body is a fight still going on in philosophy departments and I'm not equipped to settle it here. What stopped me is the honesty underneath the question. Strip away everything you can point to about yourself, your job, your language, your body, your name, your family's expectations, the country you were born in versus the one you live in now, and is there still someone there? I grew up straddling three languages and at least two countries' worth of expectations, never fully landing in either, and I've spent a lot of nights running an amateur, less rigorous version of his experiment, trying to find the version of me underneath all the labels I got handed before I could evaluate any of them. His answer is yes, there's something underneath. Not nothing. Not just the sum of the labels. A someone, floating, aware, before any of the furniture gets added to the room.

The medical legacy is real, but it should not crowd out the philosophy. The Book of Healing includes logic, natural philosophy and metaphysics; the title does not make it a second medical handbook. Reading Ibn Sina only as the author of the Canon misses a large part of his work.

The philosophical inheritance crossed languages and religious traditions through translations and argument. Ibn Sina influenced medieval Latin readers as well as later thinkers in the Islamic world. That is already enough reason to read him. We do not need to turn him into an earlier Descartes, or make a sweeping claim about every philosophy syllabus, to give him his place.

He died in 1037, leaving an extensive body of work. The uneven ways that work has been remembered are worth questioning. So is the thought experiment itself: what, exactly, have we shown when someone can affirm a self without picturing a body?

Strip away everything you can point to. Something is still floating there, awake, and it was never built from the things you lost.

KEEP THE QUESTION OPEN

A distinction worth keeping.

The Flying Man is a thought experiment, not an experiment on an actual person. Its account of immediate self-awareness does not by itself settle every question about the soul’s independence from the body. The comparison with Descartes is useful without making their arguments identical.
