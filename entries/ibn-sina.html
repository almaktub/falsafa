Thinker · Bukhara/Persia, 980–1037 CE

Ibn Sina, and the Man Floating in Nothing

Key text: Kitab al-Shifa (The Book of Healing), the Flying Man argument
Self & Consciousness Meaning

I want to start with the name of this whole site, because it's the point of this entry too. Falsafa is the Arabic word for philosophy, borrowed straight from the Greek philosophia, because when the Islamic world inherited Aristotle and Plato in the eighth and ninth centuries, they didn't just translate the texts, they kept building. For roughly five hundred years, while a lot of Europe was in what gets casually called the Dark Ages, the intellectual center of gravity for logic, medicine, astronomy, and philosophy ran through Baghdad, Bukhara, Cairo, and Cordoba. Most people who took a philosophy elective never hear a single name from that stretch. I didn't either, until I went looking on my own, which tells you something about which stories get kept and which get quietly dropped from the syllabus. Ibn Sina, known in the Latin West as Avicenna, is the biggest name from that world that almost nobody outside it knows, and once you know what he actually argued, that's a real loss.

Lived980–1037 CE, Bukhara and greater Persia (56 years)
Known forThe Canon of Medicine (Europe's standard medical text for roughly 600 years) and Kitab al-Shifa, The Book of Healing
PioneeredThe Flying Man argument on self-awareness, six centuries before Descartes' cogito, plus a systemized version of Aristotelian logic that shaped philosophy in both the Islamic world and, later, medieval Europe

Quick facts first, because they're absurd enough to need stating plainly. He had memorized the entire Quran by age ten. He'd surpassed his teachers in medicine by his late teens. He wrote that medical encyclopedia, the Canon, while also working as a court physician and vizier for a rotating cast of unstable rulers, occasionally getting imprisoned, occasionally going into hiding, writing some of his most important philosophical work from memory because he didn't have access to his library. If your idea of a philosopher is a guy calmly pacing a garden, Ibn Sina is closer to someone writing his thesis between assassination attempts.

Here's the actual argument, and it's the reason he's here and not just a history footnote. About six hundred years before Descartes sat by his fire and decided the one thing he couldn't doubt was that he was doubting, therefore he existed, Ibn Sina ran a version of the same experiment, and I think his is stranger, and honestly more convincing. Picture a person created instantly, fully grown, fully rational, but suspended in total darkness, floating, with no sensory contact with anything at all. No sight, nothing to see. No touch, his limbs don't touch each other or anything else. No sound, no smell, nothing. Total sensory blankness. This is the Flying Man.

Would this man, in that state, know that he exists?

Ibn Sina says yes, immediately, no hesitation, and that's the whole argument. Even stripped of every sense, every piece of information about his body or surroundings or history, he'd still be aware of himself, aware of his own awareness, aware there's a someone doing the not-sensing. That awareness isn't built from sensory data, because he has none. It's not inferred from evidence, because there's nothing to infer from. It's just there, immediately, the one thing that can't be stripped away because it's the thing doing the stripping. His conclusion: the self, the soul, whatever you want to call the thing that is you, isn't identical to the body. You could lose every sense, every physical reference point, and the core of you stays intact, because that core was never made of the body to begin with.

The Flying Man wasn't a standalone party trick either, it sat inside a much larger logical system he'd built out of his own reworking of Aristotle. Ibn Sina spent real effort tightening and extending Aristotelian logic, refining how syllogisms handle modal claims, statements about what's necessary, possible, or impossible rather than just what's flatly true or false, work precise enough that later logicians in both the Islamic world and, once translated, medieval Europe treated his versions as improvements on the Greek original rather than mere commentary. The Flying Man is what that logical rigor looks like when pointed at the self instead of at syllogisms, the same insistence on finding the one thing that survives every possible stripping-away, applied to the hardest object there is.

I read this the first time on a break at work, sitting in a customer's driveway between jobs, and it stopped me completely. Not because I necessarily buy the metaphysics, whether the soul's really separable from the body is a fight still going on in philosophy departments and I'm not equipped to settle it here. What stopped me is the honesty underneath the question. Strip away everything you can point to about yourself, your job, your language, your body, your name, your family's expectations, the country you were born in versus the one you live in now, and is there still someone there? I grew up straddling three languages and at least two countries' worth of expectations, never fully landing in either, and I've spent a lot of nights running an amateur, less rigorous version of his experiment, trying to find the version of me underneath all the labels I got handed before I could evaluate any of them. His answer is yes, there's something underneath. Not nothing. Not just the sum of the labels. A someone, floating, aware, before any of the furniture gets added to the room.

Here's the part that stings a little, and it's the misunderstanding this entry exists to fix. In the West, when he's remembered at all, he's remembered as Avicenna the physician, the Canon guy, the six-hundred-year medical bestseller. That's real and it's genuinely huge. But it's not how he saw himself. He considered the Book of Healing, the sprawling philosophical work that contains the Flying Man and his logic and his metaphysics, his actual life's work. Medicine, by his own account, was almost the easy stuff, something he picked up quickly and kept practicing because rulers kept needing a court physician and it paid the bills. History flattened him into the field that was more legible to later readers and buried the field he cared about more. It's a little like being remembered forever as "that guy who was pretty good at his day job" when the thing you actually poured yourself into barely made the footnote.

The other reason I wanted him here, honestly, is what he represents just by existing on this page. Falsafa itself is a thousand-year-old bridge between Athens and Baghdad and Bukhara, carried by men like Ibn Sina who read Aristotle in translation and pushed the questions further instead of just archiving them. That inheritance moved back into Europe through Latin translations of his and others' work and helped light the fuse for the scholastic philosophy that eventually produced Aquinas, and later, centuries down the line, Descartes running a thought experiment Ibn Sina had already run. None of that erases the fact that most philosophy syllabi still jump straight from Aristotle to Aquinas like nothing happened in the five hundred years and several civilizations in between. Something happened. A lot happened. This entry's a small correction, and I plan on writing more of them.

He died at fifty-six, worn down by the same instability that shaped most of his working life, having written somewhere around 450 treatises, fewer than half of which survive. Most people will never hear his name. The ones who studied medicine in Europe for six centuries were reading him without necessarily knowing where the ideas came from. That's usually how it goes with the parts of history that get quietly cropped out of frame. The floating man doesn't need the frame, though. He was never made of it to begin with.

Strip away everything you can point to. Something is still floating there, awake, and it was never built from the things you lost.
