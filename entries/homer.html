Homer, and the Choice Achilles Could Not Settle

Key text: The Iliad
About 5 minutes · A personal reading
Meaning Death
Livedc. 8th century BCE, likely Ionia, though the dates, the identity, and even whether "Homer" was one person are all genuinely disputed
Known forThe Iliad, The Odyssey
Central ideaNothing philosophy would technically call its own, he predates the word by centuries, and yet the first great philosopher spent real energy arguing against him

A QUESTION TO READ WITH

Is a remembered life worth more than a long one?

Let me be honest about why this one's here, because it's a fair question. Homer isn't a philosopher. He might not even be one person, the "Homeric Question" has occupied classicists for centuries over whether the Iliad and the Odyssey came from a single poet or a long oral tradition compressed into one convenient name by later generations. There's no argument here to reconstruct the way there is with Confucius or Nagarjuna. There's a poem. So why does a philosophy site have an entry for him.

Part of the answer is technical, and it's genuinely strange once you sit with it. A classicist named Milman Parry spent the early twentieth century studying still-living oral bards in the Balkans who composed epic-length poems from memory, night after night, never reciting the same version twice word for word, and found they relied on a toolkit of fixed phrases, "swift-footed Achilles," "wine-dark sea," "rosy-fingered dawn," that could be slotted into a line of verse to fit the meter on the fly. Parry then found the exact same pattern saturating the Iliad and the Odyssey, and concluded both poems weren't composed the way a novelist composes, alone at a desk, but assembled live, out loud, by a tradition of performers working with a shared stock of building blocks passed down for generations before anyone wrote any of it down. "Homer" may be less a single genius and more the name eventually stapled onto the last, best performance of a tradition that had been composing itself for centuries. That doesn't make the poems less remarkable. It makes them a different kind of remarkable, a group mind working in real time rather than one person's private vision.

Because Plato couldn't leave him alone, and that tells you something worth knowing. In Book Ten of the Republic, Plato does something almost nobody expects the first time they read it: he proposes banning most poetry, Homer specifically named, from his ideal city. Not because the poems are bad art, he clearly respects the craft, he even calls Homer the educator of Greece. He wants him out because poetry moves people through emotion and vivid imagery rather than through reasoned argument, and Plato thought a city trained on that kind of persuasion would end up ruled by whoever tells the most gripping story rather than whoever's actually right. He names the tension directly, "the ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry," treating it as already old news by his own time. Plato built part of his system in direct opposition to a poet who'd already been dead for centuries. That's not nothing. That's philosophy admitting where it came from and then trying to argue its way past it.

In book 9 of the Iliad, Achilles describes two possible fates while already at Troy: stay and win lasting glory at the cost of a homecoming, or return home and live longer without that glory. He does not simply select the heroic answer once and leave the matter settled. His refusal to fight, and later the death of Patroclus, make the choice change its weight. The poem lets us feel what an abstract comparison of two lives leaves out.

Homer isn't only arguing one side of that either, which is easy to miss if you only ever read the Iliad. The Odyssey, the other half of the same tradition, spends ten years following a man who already has his glory, already fought at Troy, and now wants nothing more than the long, unglamorous, unheroic road home to a wife, a son, and a kingdom he has to earn back by patience and cunning rather than by dying well. Odysseus is offered actual immortality by the goddess Calypso, an eternity with her on a private island, no death, no aging, and he turns it down to keep trying to get back to a mortal wife and a home that will eventually crumble like everything else. Achilles chooses glory over duration. Odysseus, given the literal option of never dying at all, chooses a shorter mortal life anchored to a specific family and place instead. Same tradition, same author question mark, two completely different answers to what makes a life worth having, sitting side by side in the foundational texts of an entire literature.

Later philosophical questions about meaning, mortality and risk can be read alongside Achilles’ dilemma. That comparison is not a claim that every tradition descends from Homer. Camus' Sisyphus pushing the boulder anyway. Nietzsche asking if you'd relive your exact life again. Socrates choosing the trial over exile. The comparison is illuminating precisely because their answers are different.

I left a country as a kid, obviously not by choice at that age, but I've made smaller versions of Achilles' actual choice as an adult more than once, since. There's always a safer, more forgettable path sitting right there, keep the head down, don't take the risk, don't say the thing, don't leave the comfortable job for the uncertain one. And there's the other path, louder, riskier, more likely to cost you something real, but the only one that actually feels like yours when you're doing it. Achilles didn't have Camus or Nietzsche to explain the choice to him. He just made it, three thousand years before anyone had the vocabulary to explain why it was hard.

Philosophy spent the next two and a half thousand years building arguments for a choice a poet had already dramatized without needing a single one of them.

KEEP THE QUESTION OPEN

A distinction worth keeping.

Achilles’ choices change across the poems. In Iliad 9 he still considers going home; in Odyssey 11 the dead Achilles speaks bitterly about death. The tradition leaves the value of heroic glory under pressure rather than resolving it with one brave decision.
