Siddhartha Gautama, and the Two Wrong Answers He Tried First

Key text: no self-authored text — teachings preserved orally, later compiled as the Pali Canon
Suffering Death Control
Livedc. 563–483 BCE (traditional dates, debated), Lumbini and the Ganges plain
Known forThe Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, the Middle Way
PioneeredTreating suffering itself as the subject of a philosophical system, not a problem to explain away on the road to something else

He got the answer wrong twice before he got it right, and both wrong answers are the two things most of us are still cycling between right now. That's the part of the story that gets softened into a nice palace-to-enlightenment arc, when the actual shape of it is a guy trying the extremes on purpose and finding out neither one worked.

Born a prince, kept deliberately inside the palace walls by a father who'd heard a prophecy and wanted to keep suffering, sickness, aging, and death out of his son's sight entirely. Wrong answer number one, tried before Siddhartha even chose it: total insulation. Pleasure on demand, every want met before it fully formed, nothing unpleasant ever allowed to reach him. It worked for a while, in the sense that ignorance always works for a while. Then he got out, saw an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a wandering ascetic, the four sights, and the palace's whole design collapsed at once. You cannot indulge your way around the fact that you're going to age, get sick, and die. He left that night.

Wrong answer number two came right after, and it's the one that gets skipped most often because it's less flattering to the eventual hero. He swung completely the other direction. Joined ascetics who believed the way through suffering was to out-suffer it, self-denial as a discipline, starving himself for years to the point where later accounts describe him able to feel his spine by pressing his stomach. He nearly died doing this. And he got no closer to an answer, just weaker and closer to the same death he was trying to understand in the first place. He'd overcorrected from one extreme straight into its opposite, and the opposite extreme was just as wrong, only slower and more painful to find out.

What he landed on instead, sitting under a tree with no more grand plan than to just work it out directly, was the middle way, and the phrase gets used so casually now it's worth being specific about what it actually claims. Not moderation as a vague vibe, not "everything in balance" as a fridge magnet. A precise rejection of both extremes he'd personally already tried and personally already failed at. Total indulgence doesn't touch the problem. Total self-punishment doesn't either. The actual work happens somewhere he hadn't tried yet, sustained attention to what's actually happening, rather than either drowning it out or starving it out.

From there, the Four Noble Truths: suffering is real and it's universal, not a personal failing (dukkha). It has an identifiable cause, specifically craving and attachment, wanting things to be other than they are (tanha). It can end. And there's an actual path to end it, the Eightfold Path, not a single insight but a set of ongoing practices. It's a diagnosis and a treatment plan built by someone who'd already tried both of the tempting shortcuts and personally verified neither one worked.

My trade runs on exactly this same false choice more than I'd like. There's a version of doing the job where you grind, extra hours, extra trucks, saying yes to every call because saying no feels like leaving money on the table, until you're running on fumes and hating the work that used to mean something. And there's the opposite failure mode, checking out entirely, doing the minimum, letting the whole thing go slack. Neither one is sustainable, and I've tried both longer than I should have before admitting it. The middle way isn't the compromise position between two bad options. It's the actual answer, found by someone who already burned through the two easy ones.

He tried numbing it and he tried starving it. The answer was neither, and it took nearly dying twice to find that out.
