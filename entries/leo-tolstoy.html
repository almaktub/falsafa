Tolstoy, and the Rope He Hid From Himself

Key text: A Confession (first published uncensored in 1884)
About 5 minutes · A personal reading
Meaning Death Suffering
Lived1828–1910, Russia (82 years, died in a rural train station after fleeing his own house)
Known forWar and Peace, Anna Karenina, then a full break into religious and moral writing
Central ideaTurning his own midlife collapse into a piece of philosophy instead of hiding it

A QUESTION TO READ WITH

What can success fail to answer?

Picture the most successful writer in the world at fifty. Two novels that will still be assigned two centuries later. A title, a family, an estate, and literary success. Every measurable thing a life is supposed to add up to, added up. Now picture that man removing the rope from his own study and hiding his hunting rifle, because he no longer trusted himself alone with either one. That's Tolstoy, right after Anna Karenina, and it's the least talked-about part of his career because it doesn't fit the portrait on the book jacket.

He wrote it all down plainly in a short book called A Confession, and it might be the most honest thing a famous person has ever published about wanting to die while having, by every external measure, nothing to complain about. He calls it "the arrest of life," a stopping he couldn't explain to his family or himself. He'd ask a question that sounds almost embarrassingly simple and couldn't shake it: what is the point of anything I do, given that I am going to die and the estate, the books, the children's children, all of it gets erased eventually along with everyone who remembers it. Not a mood. A question he couldn't out-argue.

What makes this more than a rich man's crisis is who he checked it against. He didn't trust his own despair, so he went looking at everyone else's answer. Science, he found, is honest about the small questions and mute on the big one, it can tell you how a candle burns but not why you should bother lighting it. Philosophy, the kind he had access to, mostly restated the problem in fancier language and called that progress. In chapter VII he describes four responses among people of his own circle: ignorance, pleasure, suicide, and continuing to live despite the question. These are categories in his account of despair, not a judgment the reader has to accept. He respected almost no one's coping mechanism, including at various points his own.

Then he looked somewhere his class had trained him not to look, which was down. The peasants on his own estate, the people whose labor built the world he was busy despairing inside of, seemed, in his account, to live with a confidence he had lost. That was his reading of them, not proof that poor people did not experience despair. They worked, buried their children, kept faith, kept working, without the luxury of treating the whole enterprise as an open philosophical wound. Tolstoy's conclusion wasn't romantic poverty tourism, though it can read that way if you're not careful. It was narrower and stranger: meaning wasn't a conclusion you reasoned your way toward from a desk. It was something you got access to by participating in something bigger than your own analysis of it, faith, labor, a family, a village, whether or not you could defend the logic on paper. The people with the least philosophy had the most functioning answer.

One consequence of that late-life turn outlived him in a way Tolstoy never could have arranged on purpose. A young Indian lawyer working in South Africa named Mohandas Gandhi read Tolstoy's late writing on nonviolence, particularly The Kingdom of God Is Within You, and later corresponded directly with him in the final year of Tolstoy's life about resisting unjust law without meeting it with violence. Gandhi credited that reading as one of the direct influences behind satyagraha, the nonviolent resistance strategy he later built an independence movement around. A Russian count's private, half-successful attempt to actually live out his own excommunicated Christianity ended up, secondhand, helping shape how an empire got dismantled on the other side of the world.

He didn't return to the Orthodox Church he'd been raised in so much as build his own severe, moralized version of Christianity, one that eventually got him excommunicated for rejecting most of its official machinery while keeping its ethical core: give things away, live simply, refuse violence, refuse the state's claim on your obedience. He spent his last three decades trying, badly and sincerely, to actually live that out inside a household that didn't want to give up the estate, the royalties, or the servants. The gap between the man writing "own nothing" and the man who owned a great deal became its own kind of torment. He finally walked out on his family in the middle of the night at eighty-two, got as far as a small rural train station, caught pneumonia, and died there, having spent his final hours trying to outrun the exact contradiction the whole crisis had been about.

I keep a version of his question on a low simmer whenever I'm running cable through some enormous house for a client I'll never see socially, thinking about how none of this, the surround sound, the automated blinds, the whole intricately wired shell of a life, survives the person living in it. I used to find that thought only depressing. Tolstoy is who taught me it can also be a filter. If a thing doesn't survive being looked at through "I will die and this will be forgotten," maybe it wasn't holding much weight to begin with. What's left after that filter, work, people, the specific unglamorous act of being useful to someone today, is smaller than the estate. It's also the only part he ever found that didn't collapse under the question.

He hid the rope from himself and then spent thirty years building a reason not to need it. That's not weakness. That's the only real project there is.

KEEP THE QUESTION OPEN

A distinction worth keeping.

A Confession reports Tolstoy’s crisis and changing judgments. Its portrayal of peasant faith is his interpretation of other people’s lives, not direct access to their inner experience. His answer is religious and ethical; reducing it to useful work alone leaves part of the argument behind.
