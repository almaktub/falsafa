Socrates, and the Trial He Could Have Walked Away From

Key text: no self-authored text — known through Plato's dialogues, especially the Apology and Crito
About 4 minutes · A personal reading
Death Meaning Control
Livedc. 470–399 BCE, Athens (roughly 71 years, ending by hemlock)
Known forThe Socratic method, "the unexamined life is not worth living"
Central ideaPhilosophy as something you do out loud, in public, at personal cost, rather than a body of doctrine you file away

A QUESTION TO READ WITH

When should loyalty to a principle become disobedience?

Socrates never wrote anything down. Everything we have of him comes secondhand, mostly through his student Plato, which means we're reading a portrait rather than a self-portrait, and scholars still argue over how much is the real man versus Plato using him as a mouthpiece. What survives clearly enough, across multiple independent sources, is the method and the ending, and the ending is the part I think about most.

His whole practice was simple to describe and apparently maddening to be on the receiving end of. He'd find someone with a public reputation for expertise, a general who supposedly understood courage, a poet who supposedly understood beauty, a politician who supposedly understood justice, and he'd ask them, patiently, to define the thing they were famous for knowing. Then he'd keep asking follow-up questions until the definition fell apart under its own contradictions. Not to humiliate them for sport, at least not by his own account, but because he genuinely believed most people, including himself, walked around assuming they understood things they'd never actually examined closely, and that unexamined confidence was more dangerous than admitted ignorance. "The unexamined life is not worth living" is the line everyone quotes, and it's worth remembering he said it at his own trial, defending a lifetime of doing exactly that to some of the most powerful men in Athens.

That habit made him beloved by young men who liked watching authority figures squirm, and it made him hated by the authority figures doing the squirming. At seventy, he was put on trial for corrupting the youth of Athens and for impiety, not properly honoring the city's gods. A jury of several hundred Athenian citizens convicted him and sentenced him to death.

The charges land differently once you know the politics sitting underneath them. Athens had just come through a brutal, short-lived oligarchy, the Thirty Tyrants, installed after Sparta defeated Athens in war, and Critias, a leader of the Thirty, and Charmides, associated with their administration in the Piraeus, had been part of Socrates’ circle. Democracy had only recently been restored by the time of his trial, and part of what some interpretations of the trial emphasize, underneath the official language about impiety, was the memory of a beloved teacher whose former students had helped install a violent dictatorship. Socrates never joined the Thirty and had in fact publicly defied one of their orders at real personal risk, but guilt by association doesn't require actual guilt, just proximity and bad timing.

He also claimed, throughout his life, to be guided by a daimonion, an inner divine voice that never told him what to do but reliably warned him whenever he was about to do something wrong, and he treated its total silence on the morning of his trial as meaningful, proof that facing death was not, in fact, the wrong path for him to take. That, paired with his famous claim to know only that he knows nothing, the Socratic paradox, the wisest man in Athens according to the Oracle at Delphi turning out to be the one person willing to admit the size of his own ignorance, is the same character showing up twice: a man who trusted an inward signal over the crowd's verdict, right up to the hemlock.

Here's the part most people skip past. He didn't have to die. In Plato’s Crito, his friend argues that escape can be arranged and that money and safe refuge need not stand in the way. This is our source’s account, not an independently verified record of every arrangement. Socrates said no. His argument, delivered through the personified voice of the Laws of Athens themselves in that dialogue, was that he'd spent his entire life as a citizen benefiting from those laws, raised under them, protected by them, free to leave the city at any point if he'd disagreed with its terms and never had, and that accepting the benefits of the system for seventy years while rejecting its verdict the one time it went against him would be a deeper injustice than the unjust death itself. He drank the hemlock surrounded by his students, reportedly calm enough to gently scold them for weeping.

I don't know that I fully buy the argument, plenty of serious readers over the centuries haven't either, there's a real case that a corrupt verdict doesn't deserve that kind of loyalty. But I keep coming back to the fact that he had the exit available and chose the harder, costlier consistency instead, on a matter of principle he'd have to actually die to prove he meant. Most of us never get tested that precisely. We get smaller versions, a chance to quietly bend a rule nobody would ever catch, a shortcut on a job that costs the client nothing they'd notice, a convenient exception to a standard we'd otherwise defend loudly. Socrates is the extreme case of what it costs to actually mean the thing you've been saying the whole time, rather than meaning it only until it becomes inconvenient.

The door was open. He stayed anyway, because he could not reconcile leaving with the argument he was prepared to defend.

KEEP THE QUESTION OPEN

A distinction worth keeping.

Plato’s dialogues give us a literary portrait of Socrates. Crito’s argument against escape sits beside Apology’s refusal to abandon philosophy on command. Reading both complicates the idea that consistency always means obeying the state.
